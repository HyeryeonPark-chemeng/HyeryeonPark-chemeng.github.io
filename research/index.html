Controlled Surface Segregation of Nanoparticles in Polymer Thin Films

Jun 2024 – Present · Nissan Chemical Korea (NCK)

Developed a single-layer impact-resistant pressure-sensitive adhesive by controlling nanoparticle localization near the film surface. I investigated how silica surface chemistry and thermal processing influence through-thickness particle redistribution and related surface enrichment to interfacial energetics, impact resistance, adhesion, and optical transparency.

Methods / Focus: Surface functionalization · Interfacial energetics · Through-thickness particle analysis · Thermal processing · Impact resistance

Research output: Korean patent application filed (2026)

Pressure-sensitive adhesives for foldable displays must simultaneously provide optical transparency, adhesion, mechanical compliance, and resistance to localized impact. This work explores whether selective nanoparticle enrichment near the film surface can create a mechanically reinforced region within a single adhesive layer, reducing reliance on multilayer architectures and their additional interfaces.

Silica nanoparticles were sequentially functionalized with GPTMS and PPGMA to tune their surface chemistry and incorporated into a low-viscosity acrylic PSA designed to permit particle redistribution during thermal treatment. OWRK-based surface-energy analysis and cross-sectional SEM/EDS profiling were combined to relate surface chemistry, processing temperature, and particle loading to through-thickness particle localization.

The results demonstrate that nanoparticle localization can be tuned through surface chemistry and thermal processing, enabling spatially selective reinforcement within a single polymer layer. Increased surface enrichment improved impact resistance while revealing trade-offs with adhesion and optical transparency. Ongoing work is examining the relative contributions of interfacial energetics and thermally driven particle transport to the observed redistribution.

Shear-Directed Nanorod Alignment in Polymer Adhesive Films

Jun 2026 – Present · Samsung Display

Investigating how shear flow can be used to control nanorod orientation in pressure-sensitive adhesive films. The project examines relationships among processing conditions, particle alignment, and the resulting mechanical and optical properties of polymer adhesive films.

Methods / Focus: Nanorod synthesis · Surface functionalization · Rheology · Shear-induced alignment · Structure–property relationships

Pressure-sensitive adhesive films for display applications must balance mechanical performance with optical transparency and interfacial adhesion. This project investigates whether directional organization of anisotropic nanoparticles can provide an additional means of controlling film properties without substantially increasing filler content.

Nanorods are incorporated into acrylic adhesive matrices, and shear during film processing is used to control their orientation. The resulting particle alignment is characterized under different processing conditions to examine how flow-induced organization develops within the polymer matrix.

The project aims to establish relationships among processing conditions, nanorod orientation, and the resulting mechanical and optical properties of the adhesive films.

Long-Term Reliability of Adhesive Interfaces in Fuel-Cell MEAs

Jul 2024 – Present · Hyundai Motor Group

Investigated the long-term durability of adhesive interfaces in proton-exchange-membrane fuel-cell assemblies under accelerated operating conditions. I designed multi-stress degradation protocols combining electrochemical, chemical, and thermal stresses and analyzed time-dependent changes in interfacial adhesion and failure mode to identify mechanisms governing sealing reliability.

Methods / Focus: Accelerated degradation · Interfacial adhesion · Multi-stress reliability · Failure-mode analysis · Polymer interface characterization

  • Papers

Reliable sealing is essential to maintaining mechanical integrity and stable operation in proton-exchange-membrane fuel cells, yet adhesive interfaces are exposed simultaneously to electrochemical, chemical, and thermal stresses during service. This work examines how these coupled environments alter interfacial adhesion and shift failure pathways over extended operation.

Accelerated aging protocols were designed to isolate and combine electrochemical, acidic, radical-induced, and temperature-cycling stresses relevant to fuel-cell operation. Time-resolved peel testing was integrated with FT-IR, AFM, FE-SEM/EDS, contact-angle, and water-uptake analyses to track changes in adhesive and membrane interfaces and distinguish transitions in failure mode over prolonged exposure.

The study revealed that sealing degradation evolves through distinct interfacial failure regimes rather than following a single degradation pathway. Electrochemical, chemical, and thermal stresses interact to alter both adhesive–membrane integrity and membrane stability over time, highlighting the need for multifactorial reliability evaluation when designing durable polymer interfaces for fuel-cell systems.

Dual-Network Acrylic Adhesives for Mechanical Recovery

May 2026 – Aug 2026

Developed dual-network acrylic adhesive films that combine a nanoparticle-mediated nanoscale elastic network with molecular-scale bridge crosslinking to improve mechanical recovery under repeated deformation. By separating the roles of elastic nano-crosslinkers and molecular-bridge crosslinkers, I established structure–property relationships linking network architecture to strain recovery, stress relaxation, adhesion, and optical transparency.

Methods / Focus: Elastic nano-crosslinkers · Dual-network design · Stress relaxation · Strain recovery · Optical/adhesive performance

  • Papers

Optical adhesive films for flexible displays must maintain transparency and interfacial adhesion while repeatedly recovering from mechanical deformation. Conventional crosslinking can improve elasticity but often restricts polymer-chain mobility, creating trade-offs among recovery, adhesion, stress relaxation, and optical uniformity. This project investigates whether hierarchical network design can balance these competing requirements.

Photoreactive elastic nano-crosslinkers with a silica core and flexible polymer shell were incorporated into an acrylic matrix to form a nanoscale elastic network, while a low-molecular-weight molecular-bridge crosslinker was introduced to enhance network connectivity. By comparing nano-crosslinker-only, molecular-crosslinker-only, and dual-network films, the individual and synergistic contributions of each network component were evaluated through adhesion, tensile, stress-relaxation, strain-recovery, cyclic loading–unloading, and optical measurements.

The dual-network architecture enabled mechanical recovery and cyclic stability to be improved without sacrificing the optical clarity and adhesion required for display applications. The results show that recovery performance is governed not simply by crosslink density, but by how nanoscale elastic reinforcement and molecular-scale connectivity cooperate to store, transfer, and recover deformation energy.
