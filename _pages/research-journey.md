  <div class="journey-intro">
    <p>Research was never an unfamiliar way of life to me; I had grown up seeing it up close. But that did not mean I assumed I would become a researcher myself. It was only after spending several years engaging with chemistry in a different way, returning fully to my studies, discovering polymers, and encountering results that challenged my own hypotheses that I began to understand why I wanted to choose research for myself.</p>
  </div>

  <section class="journey-section journey-section--split" aria-labelledby="seeing-research-heading">
    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">01</span>
      <h2 id="seeing-research-heading">Seeing a Life in Research</h2>
      <p>I first understood what a life in research could look like through my mother. She earned a Ph.D. in pharmacy and had already begun her research career before I was born, establishing herself in both research and industry at a time when professional advancement for women in Korea was far more difficult than it is today.</p>
      <p>She was always studying, even on weekends, and continued learning throughout her career. Over time, she moved into leadership roles despite many of the barriers women faced in the workplace. Growing up around her, I naturally came to admire the life of a researcher—or, more precisely, the way she chose to live and work.</p>
      <p>Still, I did not think that I wanted to become a researcher myself. Research was simply a familiar path, one I had always seen up close, rather than one I had imagined as my own.</p>

    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">02</span>
      <h2 id="known-answers-heading">Beyond Known Answers</h2>
      <p>When I was twenty, several people close to me founded a chemistry education content company, and I joined as an early member. For the next four years, I led the chemistry team, developing and reviewing mock examination questions for Korea’s College Scholastic Ability Test within the style and scope of the Korea Institute for Curriculum and Evaluation (KICE).</p>
      <p>Over time, I became more aware of the limits of that work. Because the questions had to be error-free and remain within the scope and conventions of the official exam, there was limited room to depart from established patterns. Much of the work therefore involved creating new variations within an already defined framework.</p>
      <p>I genuinely enjoyed the work, and for a long time I thought continuing in that field would be a perfectly good path. But I gradually found myself wanting to work on a different kind of problem—not one where the answer was already known and the task was to ask it differently, but one where the problem itself had yet to be defined and the next step depended on my own reasoning.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="journey-section journey-section--split journey-section--image-left" aria-labelledby="discovering-polymers-heading">
    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">03</span>
      <h2 id="discovering-polymers-heading">Discovering Polymers</h2>
      <p>After stepping away from the company and returning my focus to academics, I took Organic Materials Engineering with Professor Jun Hyup Lee, who is now my master’s advisor, and began to engage more seriously with the properties and applications of polymers.</p>
      <p>Polymer Science had previously introduced me to fundamentals such as synthesis and molecular weight, but Organic Materials Engineering made the relationship between structure and properties much more tangible. I was particularly struck by materials such as SBS and ABS, and by the idea that composition, molecular architecture, and phase structure could be deliberately adjusted to achieve desired properties. I became drawn to polymer materials because they could be engineered for a particular purpose through deliberate control of composition, formulation, and processing.</p>
      <p>During the course, I contacted Professor Lee about joining his group and entered the laboratory after my third year. There, I encountered polymer materials for very different applications, from pressure-sensitive adhesives for displays to subgaskets for proton exchange membrane fuel cells. Seeing how design and processing could tailor polymer materials for entirely different functions turned my interest in polymers into an interest in research itself.</p>

    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">04</span>
      <h2 id="research-became-mine-heading">When Research Became Mine</h2>
      <p>My approach to studying changed during my final undergraduate year after I began working in the laboratory. Research made me realize that asking meaningful questions and designing meaningful experiments required a strong theoretical foundation, so I returned to courses I had neglected while working at the company and immersed myself in academics again. While continuing my undergraduate research, I completed 42 credits over the year and maintained a GPA above 4.0 on a 4.5 scale in both semesters.</p>
      <p>Some of my most important learning, however, came from research itself. While developing low-viscosity acrylate resin formulations, I found that small changes in monomer type or composition could substantially alter film formation, cohesion, adhesion, and crosslinking behavior. Repeated cycles of formulation, testing, and refinement showed me directly how molecular structure and reactivity translated into material properties.</p>
      <p>Through these experiences, research became more than a profession I had observed from a distance or an extension of what I had learned in class. It became a process of formulating my own questions, returning to theory when necessary, and using experiments to work toward an explanation. As my interest in processing–structure–property relationships grew, research began to feel like a path I wanted to choose for myself.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="journey-section journey-section--split" aria-labelledby="enjoying-uncertainty-heading">
    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">05</span>
      <h2 id="enjoying-uncertainty-heading">Learning to Enjoy Uncertainty</h2>
      <p>During my master’s program, the project that demanded the most thought and experimentation was my thesis research. I began with a relatively simple hypothesis: that differences in surface energy between surface-modified nanoparticles and the resin would determine particle localization. When a specifically designed particle system showed the desired behavior, I initially interpreted the result as support for that hypothesis.</p>
      <p>Further experiments, however, showed that surface energy alone could not fully explain the observed behavior. I began examining other factors, including thermally driven transport and the compatibility between grafted polymer chains and the surrounding resin. Through control experiments and comparison with related studies, I gradually narrowed the range of plausible explanations.</p>
      <p>At first, it was frustrating to find that my original hypothesis did not fully align with the data. Over time, I came to see unexpected results not simply as failures, but as evidence that something important had yet to be considered. Testing alternative explanations led me to new experimental methods, analytical approaches, and theoretical frameworks that I had not previously encountered.</p>
      <p>I no longer think that the value of research lies in confirming an initial hypothesis exactly as proposed. What draws me to research is the process of tracing unexpected results, expanding my understanding, and revising both questions and hypotheses as new evidence emerges. The more I learn, the more clearly I see what remains unknown—and that continuing process of learning is one of the strongest reasons I want to continue in research.</p>
