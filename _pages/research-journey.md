  <div class="journey-intro">
    <p>Research was never an unfamiliar way of life to me, because I grew up watching someone close to me live as a researcher. But that did not mean I assumed I would become one myself. It was only after spending several years engaging with chemistry in a different way, returning fully to my studies, discovering polymers, and encountering results that did not fit my own hypotheses that I began to understand why I wanted to pursue research for myself.</p>
  </div>

  <section class="journey-section journey-section--split" aria-labelledby="seeing-research-heading">
    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">01</span>
      <h2 id="seeing-research-heading">Seeing a Life in Research</h2>
      <p>I first came to know the life of a researcher through my mother. She earned a Ph.D. in pharmacy and had already begun her research career before I was born, building her place in both research and industry at a time when professional advancement for women in Korea was far more difficult than it is today.</p>
      <p>She was always studying, even on weekends, and continued learning throughout her career. Over time, she advanced into leadership roles despite many of the barriers women often faced in the workplace. Growing up around her, I naturally came to admire the life of a researcher—or, more precisely, the way she chose to live and work.</p>
      <p>Still, I did not think that I wanted to become a researcher myself. Research was simply a familiar path, one I had always seen up close, rather than one I had imagined as my own.</p>

    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">02</span>
      <h2 id="known-answers-heading">Beyond Known Answers</h2>
      <p>When I was twenty, several people close to me founded a chemistry education content company, and I joined as an early member. For the next four years, I led the chemistry team, developing and reviewing mock examination questions for Korea’s College Scholastic Ability Test in the style and scope of the Korea Institute for Curriculum and Evaluation (KICE).</p>
      <p>Over time, I became more aware of the limits of that work. Because the questions had to be error-free and remain within the range and format that the official exam could realistically use, there was limited room to depart from established patterns. In practice, much of the work involved creating new variations within an already defined framework.</p>
      <p>I genuinely enjoyed the work, and for a long time I thought continuing in that field would be a perfectly good path. But I gradually found myself wanting to work on a different kind of problem—not one where the answer was already known and the task was to ask it differently, but one where the problem itself had yet to be defined and the next step depended on my own reasoning.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="journey-section journey-section--split journey-section--image-left" aria-labelledby="discovering-polymers-heading">
    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">03</span>
      <h2 id="discovering-polymers-heading">Discovering Polymers</h2>
      <p>After stepping away from the company and returning my focus to academics, I took Organic Materials Engineering with Professor Jun Hyup Lee, who is now my master’s advisor, and began to engage more seriously with the properties and applications of polymers.</p>
      <p>In the previous semester, Polymer Science had introduced me to the fundamentals of polymers, including synthesis, nomenclature, and molecular weight. Organic Materials Engineering, however, made the relationship between material structure and properties much more tangible through specific examples. As an undergraduate, I was particularly struck by materials such as SBS (styrene-butadiene-styrene) and ABS (acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene). I found it fascinating that components with distinct characteristics could be integrated within a single polymer material, and that composition, molecular architecture, and phase structure could be adjusted to achieve desired properties. I became increasingly drawn to polymers as a field in which materials could be designed for a particular function through deliberate control of composition, formulation, and processing.</p>
      <p>During the course, I contacted Professor Lee about joining his group as an undergraduate researcher and entered the laboratory after completing my third year. At the time, the group was studying polymer materials for a range of applications, including pressure-sensitive adhesives for displays and subgaskets for proton exchange membrane fuel cells. Reading papers and gaining hands-on experimental experience allowed me to see more clearly how polymer materials could be tailored for entirely different functions through changes in design and processing. The interest that had begun in the classroom was gradually becoming an interest in research itself.</p>

    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">04</span>
      <h2 id="research-became-mine-heading">When Research Became Mine</h2>
      <p>My approach to studying changed during my final undergraduate year after I began working in the laboratory. Reading papers and conducting experiments made me realize that understanding a research question and designing meaningful experiments ultimately required a strong theoretical foundation. I therefore returned to courses I had not been able to focus on while working at the company, retook several when necessary, and immersed myself in academics again. While continuing my undergraduate research, I completed 42 credits during that year and maintained a GPA above 4.0 in both semesters.</p>
      <p>At the same time, some of my most important learning took place through research itself. While developing low-viscosity acrylate resin formulations, I observed how even small changes in monomer type or composition could substantially affect film formation, cohesion, adhesion, and crosslinking behavior. Some formulations failed to form stable films, while others lost adhesion because of excessive crosslinking or did not release from the substrate as intended. Repeatedly designing, testing, and refining these formulations allowed me to experience directly how differences in molecular structure and reactivity could translate into material properties.</p>
      <p>Through this process, research no longer felt like a profession I had simply observed from a distance or an extension of what I had learned in class. It became a process of formulating my own questions, returning to theory when necessary, and using experiments to work toward an explanation. As I became involved in more projects, I also developed a growing interest in the relationships among processing, structure, and properties. This was when research began to feel like a path I wanted to choose for myself.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="journey-section journey-section--split" aria-labelledby="enjoying-uncertainty-heading">
    <div class="journey-copy">
      <span class="journey-section-number">05</span>
      <h2 id="enjoying-uncertainty-heading">Learning to Enjoy Uncertainty</h2>
      <p>During my master’s program, I worked on several research projects, but the one that demanded the greatest amount of time and thought was my thesis research. I began with a relatively simple hypothesis: that differences in surface energy between surface-modified nanoparticles and the resin would determine where the particles localized. When one specifically designed particle system exhibited the desired localization behavior, I initially interpreted the result as support for that hypothesis.</p>
      <p>As the study progressed and I compared the interfacial properties more quantitatively, however, I realized that surface energy alone could not fully account for the observed behavior. I therefore began to examine other possibilities, including thermally driven transport and the compatibility between polymer chains grafted onto the particle surface and the surrounding resin. Through control experiments and further examination of related studies, I gradually narrowed the range of plausible explanations.</p>
      <p>At first, it was frustrating to find that my original hypothesis did not fully align with the experimental results. Over time, however, I came to view unexpected results not simply as failures, but as indications that important variables had not yet been considered. Evaluating alternative explanations and eliminating them one by one led me to engage with a broader range of prior studies and introduced me to experimental techniques, analytical methods, and theoretical frameworks that I had not previously encountered.</p>
      <p>I no longer think that the value of research lies in confirming an initial hypothesis exactly as it was first proposed. Rather, I find meaning in tracing the reasons behind unexpected results, expanding my understanding through papers and books, and revising both hypotheses and questions as new evidence emerges. The more research I pursue, the more I expand the boundaries of what I understand while also becoming more aware of what remains unknown. That process has become one of the strongest reasons I want to continue in research.</p>
